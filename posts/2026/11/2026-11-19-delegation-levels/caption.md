**Audience:** Engineering managers, TPMs, tech leads, architects and founders who have become the default decision-maker for their teams

## Caption

Your team isn't escalating too much. Nobody told them which decisions are theirs.

Which library. Whether to roll back. Can we drop this story. Capable teams still route every "quick question" to one person, and when that person takes two days off, decisions queue up. That isn't a skills problem. It's an undefined boundary.

Delegation isn't on or off. "You own the API" is vague: does that include the database, the deadline, the vendor? Clarity comes from naming each decision area and agreeing a level for it. Jurgen Appelo's Management 3.0 model describes seven: Tell, Sell, Consult, Agree, Advise, Inquire, Delegate, moving from "I decide" to "you decide, no report needed." Many day-to-day delivery calls can sit at Advise or Inquire.

Illustrative example: library choice inside the approved stack at Delegate, rollback during an incident at Inquire, sprint scope trade-offs at Advise, hiring at Agree, the delivery date promised to the client at Consult.

Set it up in one session: list the ten decisions that reached you most last month, have everyone pick a level privately and reveal together, discuss the widest gaps, publish the board where the team works, and answer future escalations with one question: "What level did we agree?"

Which decision keeps reaching you that shouldn't?

## Alt text (per slide)

1. Dark slide headline: Your team isn't escalating too much. Nobody told them which decisions are theirs. Subtitle: delegation isn't on or off; it has levels, and each decision needs one.
2. Every "quick question" lands on you: which library, whether to roll back, can we drop this story. You take two days off and decisions queue up; it isn't a skills problem, it's an undefined boundary.
3. Delegate decisions, not tasks. "You own the API" is vague. Clarity comes from naming each decision area and agreeing a level for it. Jurgen Appelo's Management 3.0 model describes seven levels.
4. Diagram, seven levels of delegation, each with a bar showing the manager's share shrinking and the team's share growing: 1 Tell, I decide then inform you; 2 Sell, I decide then explain why; 3 Consult, I ask your input then decide; 4 Agree, we decide together; 5 Advise, I advise, you decide; 6 Inquire, you decide then tell me; 7 Delegate, you decide, no report needed. Levels 5 and 6 highlighted. Note: many day-to-day delivery calls can sit at 5 or 6.
5. Illustrative delegation board: library choice inside the approved stack, 7 Delegate; rollback during an incident, 6 Inquire; sprint scope trade-offs, 5 Advise; hiring a new engineer, 4 Agree; delivery date promised to the client, 3 Consult; team restructure, 2 Sell. Your levels will differ; the point is that they're written down.
6. Set it up in one 45-minute session: 01 list the ten decisions that reached you most last month; 02 vote privately on a level, then reveal at once; 03 discuss the widest gaps; 04 publish the board where the team works; 05 redirect escalations with one question, what level did we agree?
7. Remember this: delegation is set per decision, not per person; escalation volume is a design signal, look at the boundary before the people; agree the level before the decision arrives, not in the middle of it.
8. Question: Which decision keeps reaching you that shouldn't? And what level would you give it if you wrote it down today? Plus save and follow prompts.
