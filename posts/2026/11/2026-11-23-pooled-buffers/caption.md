**Audience:** TPMs, delivery leads, engineering managers and founders who build and defend delivery plans

## Caption

Padding every task doesn't protect your deadline. It gets spent.

Everyone on the plan added a margin, and the project still ran late. That is not bad luck. Safety hidden inside individual estimates tends to disappear: tasks with plenty of time start late, work expands to fill the time it was given, and while delays pass straight downstream, early finishes are seldom reported or used. Each margin protects one task. None of them protects the date.

The alternative is to move the safety out of the tasks and into one shared buffer. Estimate each task at what it should take if things go reasonably well, then put the safety you removed at the end of the chain as a visible project buffer. Not every task overruns at once, so a pooled buffer can be smaller than the sum of the individual pads and still give more protection where it counts. This is the core idea behind Critical Chain Project Management, introduced by Eliyahu Goldratt in his 1997 book Critical Chain.

In an illustrative five-task plan: padded, each task is 6 days of work plus 4 days of margin, 50 days in total. Pooled, it is 30 days of work plus a 10-day shared buffer, 40 days in total, and a late task can draw on all ten days instead of its own four.

The practical change is in how you report. Stop tracking whether each task hit its estimate and compare buffer used with chain completed. Used less buffer than progress made? On track. Roughly equal? Prepare a recovery option. More buffer used than progress made? Act now: cut scope, add help or reset the date while it is still cheap.

Before you pool the padding: stop punishing missed task estimates, or people will quietly pad again; start tasks when they are ready and hand off when done, so early finishes actually move the next task; and limit multitasking on the critical chain, because switching spends buffer too.

Where does the safety live in your current plan: hidden in every estimate, owned by one person, or visible to the whole team?

3. The concept: move the safety out of the tasks and into one shared buffer. Estimate each task at what it should take if things go reasonably well. Put the safety you removed at the end of the chain as a visible project buffer. Not every task overruns at once, so a pooled buffer can be smaller than the sum of the pads and still protect more.
4. Diagram comparing two plans with illustrative numbers. Plan A, padding in every task: five tasks of 6 work days each followed by a 4-day grey pad, 50 days in total; each pad is private and most get used up. Plan B, one pooled buffer: five 6-day tasks followed by one blue 10-day buffer, 40 days in total; overruns draw from it. Shorter plan, more protection where it counts: a late task in B can borrow from all ten days.
5. Illustrative example, data migration. Day 21 of a 40-day plan. Chain complete: 60 percent. Buffer used: 3 of 10 days. Schema mapping overran by three days; in a padded plan that becomes a quiet debate about whose margin it was. Here it is one honest line: 60 percent done, 30 percent of the buffer used, on track.
6. Report the buffer, not the tasks. Compare how much buffer is used with how much of the chain is done. Buffer used less than progress: on track, leave the team alone. Roughly equal: watch, prepare a recovery option. Buffer used more than progress: act, cut scope, add help or reset the date now. Sharper thresholds are a team choice; the habit that matters is checking the ratio every week.
7. Before you pool the padding. Safety first: stop punishing missed task estimates, otherwise people quietly pad again. Flow: start when ready, hand off when done; an early finish is only useful if it moves the next task. Focus: limit multitasking on the critical chain; switching between tasks spends buffer too.
8. Question: Where does the safety live in your current plan? Hidden in every estimate, owned by one person, or visible to the whole team? Plus save and follow prompts.
